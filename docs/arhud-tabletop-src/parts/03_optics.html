3Light through the glass

A flat beamsplitter adds nothing to the picture but a mirror flip and a loss. What matters is where the reflection lands, how large the road appears, how bright each picture is at the eye, and what the glass's second face does to a thin line.

3.1 The coplanar condition and its check

A flat mirror puts the image of a point as far behind the mirror as the point is in front of it. With the glass's centre {{d_glass}} mm above the PGU's face and {{d_glass}} mm in front of the road monitor's face, every PGU pixel's image lies on the road monitor's face. Two consequences carry the whole design. First, there is no parallax: the overlay and the road are one plane, so the viewer may move and a mark drawn over a painted line stays over it. Second, the mapping from a road-monitor pixel to the PGU pixel whose reflection covers it is a fixed two-dimensional transformation, independent of where the eye is; for a perfect rig it is a vertical mirror at unit scale, and for the real one it is that plus a small homography that one photograph fits (chapter 8).

The condition is checked by parallax, which is far more sensitive than a rule. Draw a cross at the window's centre on the PGU and the same cross on the road monitor. From the eye point, photograph them; move the camera {{e_shift}} mm sideways and photograph again. If the planes differ by δ, the two crosses separate by {{e_shift}} × δ / {{d_img}} mm on the road monitor, {{cop_px_per_mm}} px per millimetre of δ. A tolerance of {{cop_tol_px}} px therefore holds the planes within {{cop_tol_mm}} mm, closer than a rule reads on a monitor behind glass.

3.2 The flip

Mirroring about the glass maps a PGU point at (x, y, −{{d_glass}}) in the rig frame to ({{d_glass}}, y, −x): the PGU's near edge becomes the top of the image, its far edge the bottom, and left stays left. Laid with its top edge away from the viewer (cable out the back), the PGU's picture reads upside down in the glass; hudd's warp pass carries a vertical mirror. Rotating the monitor instead would trade it for a horizontal mirror; one of the two is unavoidable, as in any single reflection. The test card of B6.2 settles it.

3.4 Luminance and contrast at the eye

On the vendor's figures, the PGU's white reaches the eye as {{l_mon}} × 0.30 = {{l_hud_eye}} cd/m², and the road monitor's white through the glass as {{l_mon}} × 0.70 = {{l_road_white}} cd/m². A noon clip's sky is that white; its sunlit asphalt sits near {{asphalt_pct}}% of it, {{l_asphalt}} cd/m² assumed. The HUD white over the clip's sky therefore gives a contrast ratio of at most {{cr_sky_max}}, and over its asphalt {{cr_asph_max}}. 3.5 Polarisation

An IPS monitor emits linearly polarised light. Plain glass at 45° reflects s-polarised light at {{rs45}}% and p-polarised at {{rp45}}% (n = 1.52, refracted angle {{theta_t45}}°), a ratio of {{sp_ratio45}}; the beamsplitter coating's split between s and p is not published for the NA17, and the "30%" is for whichever light the vendor measured. With the PGU face up and the glass tilted about a left-right axis, the plane of incidence is vertical and s is the left-right polarisation. If the panel's polariser is at 45° to its edges, as many are, the reflected share is a mix; if it is left-right, the reflection is strongest; if it is up-down, the coating may reflect far less than 30%. verify in T-G: meter the PGU's white in the glass; if it is well under 30% of its direct reading, a half-wave retarder film on the PGU (in the plan's BOM) rotates the polarisation to s, or the PGU is rotated 90° in its well with the picture rotated in software. The road monitor is unaffected: transmission at 45° differs between s and p by only a few percent.

3.6 The ghost

The glass's second face reflects too. For {{glass_t}} mm glass at 45° the two reflected rays run {{ghost_mm}} mm apart, which from {{d_img}} mm is {{ghost_arcmin}}′ or {{ghost_px}} monitor pixels, {{ghost_ratio_car}} times the plan's {{ghost_car_arcmin}}′ windshield ghost at 7.5 m. Its brightness relative to the main reflection depends on the second face: with the anti-reflection coating on that face about {{ghost_rel_ar}}% (residual reflectance 0.5%, assumed); on a bare face {{ghost_rel_s}}% for s-polarised light, {{ghost_rel_p}}% for p, {{ghost_rel_u}}% unpolarised. So the orientation of the glass matters: coating toward the PGU with the anti-reflection face toward the viewer gives a ghost {{ghost_px}} px away at under 1%, invisible on filled areas and faint on a line; the other way round gives up to a sixth of the line's brightness {{ghost_px}} px away, a visible double. The {{ghost_car_pct}}% ghost of the car's plain laminate is a different problem with a different cure (thick lines, filled areas, wedge glass), and the bench says nothing about it.

3.7 What the glass costs the picture

Nothing else: a 2 mm plate at 45° shifts the transmitted road picture by a millimetre and adds no distortion the homography cannot absorb; the window's {{win_w}} mm is well inside the glass's {{glass_foot}} mm reflected depth and {{glass_w}} mm width; the eye sees the whole road monitor through the glass (section 2.3). Keep the glass clean and the hood on: a fingerprint on either face is a haze on both pictures.
