6Optics

The optics are the hardest part of the plan, and the part the monitors alone will not get done. The project's description commits to projection onto a relay mirror. This chapter designs that path for a TFT picture generator, keeps the two cheaper stages that come before it so software is never blocked on glass, and adds the one subsystem the TFT choice makes necessary: a backlight bright enough for daylight. Each subsection explains the physics before the numbers, and Appendix A derives every formula used.

The contrast ratio in the plan is defined as (overlay + background) ÷ background. With a background of 10,000 cd/m² and a ratio of 1.15, the overlay must add 0.15 × 10,000 = 1,500 cd/m², which is where the sky target comes from. The road target is the same arithmetic at 5,000 cd/m². The definition matters because a different one, such as the ratio of overlay to background, would give a different target. Both targets are kept: the sky target is what a production HUD meets, and the road target is the floor below which the daytime demo does not work at all.

6.2 Window geometry

A field of view is an angle, and the physical size of the image depends on the distance it is drawn at. At a virtual image distance of 7.5 m, the window's width is 2 × 7.5 m × tan(θ/2), where θ is the field of view. The window's shape must match the picture generator's active region. With a TFT panel that region is a software choice: the plan lights and draws a 60 × 30 mm crop of a 5.5-inch 1920 × 1080 panel, which is 2:1, and the rest of the panel stays black. 6.3 Reflection off glass, and why the panel's orientation matters

When light meets a glass surface, part is transmitted and part is reflected, and the fraction reflected depends on the angle and on the light's polarisation. Polarisation has two components. The s component is perpendicular to the plane of incidence; the p component is parallel to it. For glass with a refractive index of 1.52, the Fresnel equations give the reflected fraction for each. Figure 13 plots them against the angle of incidence.

At the plan's design point of 65°, the s component reflects {{rs65}} of its light and the p component reflects {{rp65}}: a factor of {{rs65_x}}. For the projector-and-diffuser design this was a liability, because the diffuser scrambled the polarisation and a polariser sheet had to throw away more than half the light to recover an s-polarised beam. For a liquid-crystal panel it is an asset. A panel's front polariser transmits one linear polarisation, so the panel already emits polarised light, and all that is required is to orient that polarisation perpendicular to the plane of incidence at the windshield. The orientation is set once and costs nothing in brightness.

Three details decide whether the asset is realised.

  • The panel's axis is not where one would guess. Most panels have their front polariser at 45° to the panel's edges, some at 0° or 90°, and the datasheet rarely says. The axis is measured in Phase 5 with a known polariser and the luminance meter. If the axis is wrong for the mounting, a half-wave retarder film laid on the panel rotates it; the film is cheap and the plan buys one in any case.
  • The mirrors must preserve the s-state. A metal concave mirror at the plan's angles (under 45°) does. The fold is a dielectric cold mirror, and a dielectric stack reflects s and p differently at oblique incidence, so its plane of incidence is aligned with the windshield's and its s-reflectance is checked against the vendor's curve at the fold angle; the end-to-end check is the same measurement at the eye point.
  • Polarised sunglasses block the image. They are made to block exactly the s-polarised light that the windshield reflects best. The demo-day rule is no polarised eyewear; the production fix, p-polarised glass with a special coating, is out of scope (section 11.1).

The angle of 65° is the plan's design incidence on the windshield. For the assumed windshield rake of {{rake}}° above horizontal and a look-down of {{lookdown}}°, the incidence is {{inc_check}}° (Appendix A.12). The ray trace in Stage 2 will confirm the real angle, and if it differs a great deal the budget in section 6.5 is recomputed from figure 13.

6.4 The ghost image, and why the windshield is not replaced first

A laminated windshield has two reflecting surfaces, the inner and the outer, separated by the glass and the plastic interlayer. Each reflects the picture, so the driver sees two images. In a plain laminate the two surfaces are parallel, so the two reflected rays are parallel too, displaced sideways by a fixed distance that depends only on the glass thickness and the angle. That displacement is the ghost. A wedge-shaped interlayer tilts the inner surface by a small angle so the two reflections converge at the design eye point; that is the fix production HUDs use.

The previous plan treated the wedge windshield as a prerequisite. The geometry says it is not, for an image this far away, and Texas Instruments' application note on AR HUD optics says the same: "the wedged windshield is no longer required for AR HUDs using long virtual image distances". For an assumed windshield build of {{t_glass}} (two 2.1 mm plies and a 0.76 mm interlayer, the standard thicknesses named in the glass patents, assumed for the chosen car), the two reflected rays are {{ghost_perp}} apart. Because they are parallel, the eye sees them as two copies of the image displaced by that distance at the image, and the angular separation is the displacement divided by the image distance from the eye. The arithmetic is in Appendix A.2, where it is checked against a published worked example, and the result is in the table.

Two things follow. The base plan uses the car's own windshield and sizes its lines so the ghost reads as a soft edge: ribbons are area fills, lane lines are at least 6 pixels, and text in the speed block is set in a heavy weight. And the wedge windshield becomes a measured decision: the Stage 2 ghost photograph at the eye point is compared against this table, and the windshield is ordered only if the measured ghost is worse than predicted or judged objectionable. The decision has a second benefit, that the car no longer needs a factory-HUD trim, which widened the car choice from a list to any car with a usable dash.

Two caveats. The ghost's angular size does not shrink for the fixed speed block if that block is ever moved to a nearer plane; it is on the 7.5 m plane in this design. And the ghost is {{ghost_rel}} as bright as the main image, so when both are counted in a luminance measurement over a broad patch the windshield effectively reflects {{rs65_two}}, which section 6.5 uses as the optimistic column.

6.5 The luminance budget

Luminance, measured in cd/m², is the brightness of a surface as seen by the eye. It is conserved by an ideal imaging system: a mirror that magnifies an image makes it bigger, not brighter. So the budget is a chain of multipliers from the panel to the eye. Each reflection multiplies the panel's luminance by a fraction. Two relay mirrors at an assumed 90% each and one glass surface at {{rs65}} give a path of {{path_ws}}; with both glass surfaces counted (wedge glass, or a patch wider than the ghost offset) it is {{path_ws_two}}. The budget's question is therefore simple: how bright must the panel be?

Three readings of the table. First, no panel that can be bought as a hobbyist part reaches daylight on its own; the industrial class gets to overcast. Second, the custom backlight is the ordinary engineering answer, not an exotic one: a Continental patent specifies a virtual image of at least 5,000 cd/m² and preferably {{prod_eye_lo}} to {{prod_eye_hi}}, from a panel "five to ten times" brighter, 50,000 to 100,000 cd/m²; TI's note says AR HUDs want up to {{prod_eye_hi}} at the eye and under 3 cd/m² at night. Third, and this is the honest reading, the plan's sky target of 1,500 is a minimum-visibility threshold at a contrast ratio of 1.15, while production aims at a ratio near {{prod_ratio_sky}} against the same sky. The Lambertian backlight at {{l_panel_design}} reaches {{bl_share_prod}} of the production floor and a ratio of {{bl_ratio_sky}}; the collimated version of section 6.6 at an assumed gain of {{gain_collim}} reaches {{blc_ws}} at the eye and a ratio of {{blc_ratio_sky}}. Reaching production brightness means reaching production's panel luminance, {{need_panel_prod_lo}} cd/m² and up through this path, which only a collimated backlight does at sane power. The plan therefore builds the Lambertian backlight to clear the minimum with margin on the road target, and treats collimation as the route to a production-class image rather than as an optimisation.

Scene luminances for reference, from lighting-engineering tables: a clear sky at noon about 10,000 cd/m², an overcast sky at noon about 1,000, and sunlit asphalt {{asph_lo}}–{{asph_hi}} cd/m² from its luminance coefficient of 0.07–0.10 cd/m² per lux at 50,000–100,000 lux. The plan's 5,000 for the road target sits inside that range.

Dimming follows from the two thresholds. From the sky target at the eye to the night ceiling is {{dim_day_night}}; from the custom backlight's full output to the night ceiling is {{dim_bl_night}}. A pulse-width-dimmed LED backlight covers that range with no fixed filters, and the ambient-light sensor on the controller board sets it. The projector plan's dimming-floor risk and its neutral-density fallback do not exist here. What exists instead is the black level, covered in the next section.
