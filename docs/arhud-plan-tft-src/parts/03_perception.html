5Perception without a driving stack

This chapter replaces the self-driving chapter of the previous plan. It chooses the road sensor, the networks, and the motion source, and then does the one piece of analysis that decides whether any of it is good enough: how an error in measured range turns into a registration error at the eye. The answer, in section 5.5, is that bearing matters and range mostly does not, which is why the plan can use modest networks and a stereo camera rather than a radar.

5.1 The road sensor

The road camera has three jobs: give the networks an image, give the objects a range, and give the lane points a ground plane. A monocular camera can do the first and, with a flat-road assumption, approximate the other two. A stereo camera does all three directly, and the plan chooses the Stereolabs ZED 2i: two colour cameras on a 120 mm baseline, a built-in inertial unit, a USB 3 interface, and a software kit that runs on the Jetson and computes depth, a floor plane and visual-inertial motion on the GPU. The 4 mm lens variant is preferred: its narrower field puts more pixels on the road ahead, where the HUD window is, and gives better range at distance. The specific figures (field, rate, kit support for the Jetson's software version) are listed in chapter 8 with their sources.

The sensor is rolling-shutter, like the sensors in most driving cameras. For a forward-looking camera the skew from rolling shutter is small, and the ZED's timestamps are per frame. Mounting matters more than shutter type: the camera is mounted high on the windshield's centreline, next to the mirror stalk, so it is within about half a metre of the driver's eye. Section 5.5 shows why that distance is the number that decides registration.

Depth from stereo degrades with the square of distance. With the design assumptions of Appendix A.10 (a quarter-pixel disparity error, the 4 mm lens at 1080p) the range error is about {{sdz_10}} m at 10 m, {{sdz_20}} m at 20 m, {{sdz_30}} m at 30 m and {{sdz_50}} m at 50 m. That is {{sdz_30_pct}} at 30 m and {{sdz_50_pct}} at 50 m: good enough for a gap readout, and far better than the HUD needs for registration.

5.2 Lane lines

Lane detection networks take an image and return each lane line as a list of points in image coordinates, with a confidence per line. The plan's candidates, with their published CULane scores (an F1 figure on a Chinese road dataset, useful only for ranking), are listed below. All have public weights; two ship an export path to TensorRT, and none has a published frame rate on an Orin, so the Phase 1 measurement is the only number that counts.

The lift from image points to road points is where a monocular design goes wrong, and the plan avoids it. roadd fits a plane, by random-sample consensus, to the stereo depth in the region of road directly ahead, every frame. Each lane point from the network is a ray from the camera; its intersection with that plane is its position on the road. Because the plane is measured each frame, the car's pitch under braking and acceleration, and the road's own grade, are absorbed. The fit is the plan's own code on the depth map, not the kit's floor-plane call, because that call requires the kit's positional tracking to be running, and tracking costs about 44% of the Orin NX's GPU by the kit's own table. The fallback when the fit fails (dense traffic, wet road, night) is the calibrated static ground plane with the inertial unit's pitch estimate applied.

What the HUD draws from this: the two lines bounding the ego lane, the ribbon between them, and any further lines the network reports with confidence. The ego lane is the pair of lines with the smallest lateral offsets of opposite sign at the car. A single visible line gives a ribbon of the standard 3.7 m width, flagged as inferred by a lighter tint.

5.3 Objects, range and the lead vehicle

detectd runs a general object detector trained on COCO classes, which already include person, bicycle, motorcycle, car, bus, truck, stop sign and traffic light. The plan's first choice is YOLO11s in FP16, which Ultralytics measures at 7.9 ms per 640-pixel image on an Orin NX 16 GB with TensorRT (5.3 ms for the smaller 11n); its 8-bit variant is faster but the same table shows its accuracy collapsing on this hardware, so FP16 is the setting. The weights carry a copyleft licence (AGPL) that requires the project's code to be published, which this repository already is; if that ever changes, RF-DETR Nano (Apache 2.0, 6 ms on the same board) or NVIDIA's DashCamNet (car, bicycle, person and road sign classes, built for this hardware) are the drop-in alternatives. A tracker in the ByteTrack or OC-SORT family (MIT, CPU-only, hundreds of frames per second for the association step) gives each detection a stable identity and a velocity, so outlines do not flicker and moving objects can be predicted forward.

Range comes from the depth map: the median depth over the lower fifth of the box, which is the part most likely to be the object's own surface rather than the background. Beyond the stereo range, 35 m for the 4 mm lens by its datasheet, the range is taken from the ground plane at the box's base instead, with the larger error that section 5.5 shows is still tolerable at that distance. The lead vehicle is the nearest tracked car, bus, truck or motorcycle whose base lies inside the ego-lane ribbon. The chevron is drawn at the bearing of the box's bottom centre, at that range, on the ground.

The gap in seconds is range divided by the car's own speed. It is shown only when the speed is above 3 m/s, because at low speed it grows without meaning.

5.4 Ego-motion without the car

The age compensation of section 4.6 needs speed and yaw rate with low latency; the speed block needs speed that is right. The plan uses two instruments. A u-blox ZED-F9R dead-reckoning GNSS module gives speed from satellite Doppler, which is accurate to a few centimetres per second while moving and does not drift, fused on the module with its own inertial unit so it continues through short outages. The ZED 2i's inertial unit gives yaw rate, pitch rate and acceleration at several hundred hertz, with a latency of a few milliseconds. egod runs a small filter: GNSS speed is the slow, unbiased reference; the gyroscope supplies the fast turn rate; the accelerometer propagates speed between GNSS updates and corrects for the GNSS latency.

The ZED's own visual-inertial motion estimate is a third source, and the evidence says not to rely on it: a published benchmark of the earlier ZED 2 in a vehicle found it could not track a straight path outdoors and drifted more when moving fast, a user of the ZED 2i reported half the true distance in an empty car park, and the kit's tracking costs about 44% of the Orin NX's GPU in its current mode. It is used as a cross-check in Phase 1 only (verify).

The GNSS module's own figures: velocity accuracy of 0.05 m/s (its datasheet's figure, at 30 m/s), heading to 0.2°, fused output at up to 30 Hz, and dead reckoning at about 2% of distance through an outage. Its output latency is not specified; one user measured about 12 ms from the timing pulse to the message on a similar module. The filter in egod treats the latency as a parameter to be measured against the flashing-LED test rather than a constant.

Stationary and low speed are handled explicitly. Below 1 m/s the GNSS speed is noise; the block shows 0 and the ribbon is drawn without prediction. Without a fix, the block shows a dash and the status glyph, and the ribbon is drawn without prediction, which at low speed is harmless and at high speed is visible, which is the point.

5.5 Why range barely matters: the camera-to-eye baseline

This is the analysis that makes the rest of the chapter safe to build. An object seen in the camera has an exact bearing: the pixel it occupies. Its range is measured with some error. When the HUD draws the object for the eye, it must shift the bearing by the parallax between the camera and the eye, and that shift depends on range. A range error therefore produces a registration error of about the camera-to-eye baseline times the range error over the range squared (Appendix A.9).

Three conclusions. With the camera within about half a metre of the eye, a 10% range error stays under the 0.3° target at every distance that matters. Stereo range is an order of magnitude better than that, so the stereo camera is not the limiting instrument. And the fallback ground plane with a 1° pitch error costs {{pitch_reg}}° at 20 m, which is why the per-frame plane fit is the primary mode and the static plane only the fallback.

The same baseline argument says where not to put the camera. A camera on the dash top, 0.9 m from the eye, doubles every number in the table; one on the roof or the bumper adds a metre or more. The windshield-centre mount is a registration decision, not a convenience.

5.6 Speed-limit signs

The detector finds signs as boxes; it does not read them. A second, tiny classifier on the crop of each sign box reads the value of US speed-limit signs. Two public datasets cover US signs: LISA (47 sign types, under an academic licence) and Mapillary's traffic-sign set (research edition free for academic use, a commercial edition otherwise). Both are usable for a university demo; neither may be redistributed with a product. The classifier can be trained in an afternoon on a few thousand crops. The value is accepted after two consistent reads from a tracked sign and shown in the speed block until the next sign. This is the only element whose source is not geometric, and it is marked as a stretch within the demo: if it is not reliable on the course, the slot shows the speed alone.

5.8 GPU budget

Everything in the pipeline except the tracker and the filters runs on the Orin NX's GPU, and so does the render pass. Figure 11 lists the plan's estimates per frame. Two of them rest on published measurements for this board: Stereolabs' table puts its lighter neural depth mode at 23% of the GPU and its standard mode at 59% at 30 frames per second, which is 8 to 20 ms per frame; Ultralytics puts YOLO11s at 7.9 ms. The lane network and the render pass are assumptions, to be replaced by measurements in the first week on the Jetson.

The serial sum is {{gpu_min}}–{{gpu_max}} ms per frame. At 30 Hz the frame period is {{gpu_frame_30}} ms, so the pessimistic end does not fit and the optimistic end uses {{gpu_load_30_min}} of the GPU. At 20 Hz the period is {{gpu_frame_20}} ms and even the pessimistic end fits. The plan therefore runs the camera-rate topics at 30 Hz if the measurements allow and at 20 Hz if they do not, and takes depth at half the rate of the networks if that is what it takes: the lead chevron's bearing updates at camera rate from the image, and its range changes slowly. The decision is a Phase 1 measurement, not a design assumption.

5.9 Recordings, scoring, and the Phase 1 gate

Perception is developed from recordings, not from live drives. The ZED's kit records its stereo stream and inertial data to a file that replays through the same code as live capture, so every network and the plane fit can be run on the Jetson against the same minutes of road many times. Recordings are made from the passenger seat of any car in week 1: the demo course, the same course at dusk and at night, and an ordinary road with traffic, each with the camera at the intended mounting position.

The score is defined before the first network is trained or chosen. For lanes: on 300 hand-labelled frames of the course, the lateral error of each drawn line at 10, 20 and 30 m, in metres, and the fraction of frames where the ego lane is found. For objects: the recall of vehicles in the ego lane within 50 m, and of pedestrians within 30 m, and the range error against the stereo median. The Phase 1 gate (section 9.2) is a number on those scores, and the fallback if it fails is the passive comma four of section 5.7.
