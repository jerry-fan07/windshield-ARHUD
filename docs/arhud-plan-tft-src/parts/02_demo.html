3What the demo shows

Everything else in the plan is derived from this list. In a window above the hood line, registered to the real road, the driver sees six things. The window is {{fov2_w}}° × {{fov2_h}}° at a virtual distance of 7.5 m, which is the field the 60 × 30 mm crop of the panel gives through the relay mirror (section 6.2).

3.1 The six items

  1. Lane ribbon. A translucent ribbon lying on the road surface between the two detected lane lines of the driver's lane, from the bottom of the window out to the last confident point, typically 40 to 60 m. It is the hero element because any error in its edges is visible against the real paint. It is tinted a steady blue; it is not a prediction of where the car will go, and the plan never calls it a path.
  2. Lane lines and road edges. Thin lines over the real paint and the kerb or verge. They are drawn only where the lane network is confident, and they fade with the confidence instead of popping on and off. The thresholds are set in section 7.4.
  3. Lead vehicle marker. A chevron under the rear of the nearest vehicle in the driver's lane, with the gap in metres and in seconds at the current speed. The bearing comes from the image and the range from stereo depth, so the chevron sits under the bumper even when the range is a few per cent off (section 5.5).
  4. Speed and the last speed limit. A small fixed block low in the window: speed from the GNSS receiver, and the value of the last speed-limit sign the detector read. It is the only non-conformal content, so it is the only element that does not move with the scene. A corner of the block carries status glyphs for sensor health.
  5. Hazard cues. Pedestrians, cyclists and signs detected by the Jetson, highlighted as outlines that follow the object through the tracker. They are informational: the HUD does nothing with them except draw them.
  6. Head tracking made visible. The passenger-side monitor shows the rendered frame, the driver camera with the eye point overlaid, and the live registration and age numbers, so the audience can watch the parallax correction work as the driver leans.

3.3 What is deliberately left out

  • A drive path. There is no planner, so nothing is predicted about where the car will go. A ribbon that looked like a path but came from nothing would mislead.
  • Lane-departure warnings. They are a small addition to the ribbon (its edge could glow when the car drifts), and they are left out of the demo so the HUD is not read as a driver-assistance product. They are listed as a stretch goal.
  • Navigation arrows. They need a route, a map and map matching, which is a project of its own. Stretch goal.
  • Full-windshield clutter. Anything not conformal or safety-relevant is removed or dimmed. The clutter mode in the demo is a deliberate contrast, shown for ten seconds.

Behaviours that must be defined before any car drive

These matter for safety and are set in Phase 2, before the first drive on a monitor. Section 7.4 gives the plan's proposed values; they are proposals until tested.

  • What the HUD shows when a topic stops arriving: the stale-data cutoff per topic, and how the content fades rather than freezes.
  • The confidence at which lane lines begin and finish fading, and the minimum time a line must be seen before it is drawn.
  • The minimum confidence and persistence for a hazard outline, and how a hazard that leaves the view is removed.
  • What the speed block shows when GNSS has no fix or the car is stationary.
  • How the demo's clutter mode is defined: which elements appear, and at what brightness.

4System architecture

4.1 One computer

The architecture has one computer and three kinds of input. The Jetson reads a stereo road camera, a driver camera and a GNSS receiver, and talks to a small controller board that handles the panel's backlight, an ambient-light sensor, a temperature sensor on the panel and the sun shutter. Its HDMI output drives the panel's board. Its Ethernet port streams an audience view to a mini PC with the second monitor. Nothing else is connected to anything.

The split of the previous plan had four reasons tied to openpilot: the safety boundary, calibrated sensing, an existing transport, and a GPU with a real job. Three of them are moot without openpilot. The fourth remains: the Orin NX's GPU now carries stereo depth, a lane network, an object detector, face landmarks and the render pass, and section 5.8 budgets it.

4.2 The interfaces to the car

The safety argument has to be easy to state, so it is stated as a list of wires. The HUD's housing sits on the dash top and is held by its own base and the dust-cover frame; nothing is bonded to the glass in the base design. The road camera mounts to the windshield's inside with a removable suction or adhesive bracket near the mirror stalk. The driver camera and the GNSS antenna mount on the dash. Power comes from the car's 12 V accessory socket through a fused, ignition-sensed converter. There is no connection to the OBD port, to any CAN bus, to the body computer or to any actuator. Figure 4 says the same thing.

Two consequences. First, the acceptance criterion for the boundary is an inspection with a checklist, signed before each drive: no connector reaches a vehicle bus, every mount is removable, the fuse is in place. Second, the speed shown to the driver is not the car's speedometer value. The plan says so on the audience monitor and in the script, and the speed block shows the GNSS fix state.

4.3 The perception pipeline

Figure 5 shows the data path from camera frames to pixels. Three processes consume the road camera: laned runs the lane network on the left image and publishes lane points in the image with confidences; detectd runs the object detector and a tracker and publishes boxes with stable identities; roadd owns the camera, computes stereo depth, fits the ground plane, and lifts both the lane points and the boxes to three dimensions in the car frame. egod fuses GNSS speed with the inertial unit's gyroscope and accelerometer into a 100 Hz estimate of speed, yaw rate and pitch. hudd reads everything, compensates for the age of each topic, projects into the driver's eye, warps for the optics, and draws at 60 Hz.

The division is by rate and by failure. Lanes and objects run at camera rate and may drop frames under load without stopping each other. Ego-motion runs at the inertial rate and must not drop, because the age compensation depends on it. The renderer runs at the panel's rate and must not block on any input: it draws whatever is newest and fades whatever is stale.

4.4 Topic timing

Each topic has its own rate. The renderer does not need to match them. It keeps the latest message from each topic and the capture timestamp inside it, draws at 60 Hz regardless, and advances each message's content by its measured age. A lane message at 30 Hz is therefore drawn twice at the render rate, and each draw uses the ego-motion that has arrived since the frame was captured.

The timestamp discipline is the whole trick. A lane message describes the road as it was when the camera's shutter closed, not when the message arrived, and the car has moved since. Every topic therefore carries the capture time of the frame it was computed from, on the Jetson's monotonic clock, and the renderer computes age from that. Because everything is on one computer, there is no clock alignment between hosts, which removes a step the previous plan needed.

4.5 Latency budget

Road-registered content is convincing only if it stays put when the car moves. The relevant delay is from the moment light leaves the road to the moment the corresponding pixel lights up on the panel: motion-to-photon latency. The literature puts the perceptual threshold for head-mounted AR near 20 ms, and university-project targets for automotive HUDs near 50 ms. 4.6 Predicting forward

The image cannot be made faster than the pipeline, so the HUD predicts instead. Each lane point and object is in the car's frame at capture time, with x forward, y to the right and z down. The car has moved since, so before projection hudd moves each point to where it is now: back by the distance travelled, speed × age, and rotated by the yaw the car has turned through, yaw rate × age, both integrated from the ego topic between the capture time and now rather than taken as constants.

age = t_now − t_capture p_now = Rz(−∫ yawRate dt) · (p_capture − [∫ v dt, 0, 0]) integrals over the age

The sign follows from the car moving forward, so points move toward the car. Confirm the sign and the rotation direction on a recording before relying on either; a wrong sign doubles the error instead of removing it.

The size of the effect is easy to misjudge, so the figure shows both cases. Take a road point 10 m ahead, an eye 1.22 m above the road, and a car at 30 m/s. In 100 ms the car moves 3 m. Without prediction, the point's angle below the horizontal changes from {{dep0}}° to {{dep1}}°, a shift of {{uncomp}}° as seen by the driver. With prediction, the remaining error comes from what the motion estimate got wrong. Given 1 m/s² of unmodelled acceleration over the same 100 ms, the error is {{resid_mm}}, which is {{resid_deg}}° at 10 m. A speed error of 1 m/s over the same age gives {{resid_speed_deg}}°. The plan's targets are 0.5° static and 0.3° under head motion (section 10).

Prediction has limits. It corrects for the car's own motion. It does not correct for another road user's motion, for a lane line that the network re-estimates differently next frame, or for a sudden change in acceleration. The tracker in detectd carries a velocity per object, so moving objects are predicted too, by their own measured velocity, which is noisier than the car's. The draw region starts at the bottom edge of the virtual window, so nothing is drawn where the hood hides the road.

4.7 Head tracking and parallax

The virtual image is fixed in space, but the driver's eye moves. A graphic computed for the nominal eye position therefore lands in a different place on the road when the eye moves. The shift is called parallax. The head tracker measures where the eye is so hudd can compute the mark's position from the actual eye.

Parallax is largest for near objects. A 150 mm head shift, which is the plan's test span, moves a point 10 m away by {{par_10}}° as seen from the eye, and a point 30 m away by {{par_30}}°. The head tracker removes most of that shift when it works.

The eye box is the region where the eye can see the whole image. The plan requires at least 120 × 60 mm without tracking and designs for 130 × 70 mm. With tracking, the box follows the eye, so the test span of ±75 mm is larger than the static box and is only met with tracking on. The tracker is part of the design, not an optional extra. With a TFT panel there is one more coupling: the backlight's emission cone sets how far off-axis the image stays bright, so the cone is designed to the eye box (section 6.6).
