6.6 The TFT picture generator and its backlight

A liquid-crystal panel makes no light. It is a shutter array in front of a backlight: each pixel passes a fraction of the light behind it, set by the voltage on its cell, through colour filters and two crossed polarisers. The panel's luminance is the backlight's luminance times the cell's transmittance, and that transmittance is small, typically 5 to 8% for a colour panel (assumed {{t_lcd}} in this plan; the first measurement in Phase 5 replaces it). Everything about brightness therefore comes down to the backlight, and the plan designs one.

Which panel

The plan wants a panel that is small enough to package, dense enough for about 90 pixels per degree, available with an HDMI driver board, and cheap enough to buy two of and to take apart. The parts search found no 5-inch 1920 × 1080 kit; the nearest is a 5.5-inch 1920 × 1080 IPS kit with an HDMI board at $95 and 450 cd/m², and that is the design panel. The backlight design

The stock backlight of a 5.5-inch panel is an edge-lit light guide behind the whole panel, giving a few hundred cd/m². It is removed and replaced by a direct-lit unit behind the crop alone: a small board of high-power white LEDs on a heat sink, a mixing cavity with white walls, a diffuser plate, a pair of crossed prism films, and an infrared-cut glass, under the panel's own cell with its polarisers. The LEDs are driven from a constant-current driver with a pulse-width input, which the controller board sets from the ambient-light sensor, so the dimming range is set by the driver's minimum duty, not by the LEDs.

The emission cone is the subtle part, and it is where a TFT backlight differs from a projector screen. Figure 17 shows why. A point on the panel must send light into the cone that the mirror maps onto the eye box, and nowhere else: light outside that cone never reaches an eye. For the design eye box of 130 × 70 mm at a PGU distance of {{u2}}, the cone is ±{{cone_h}}° wide and ±{{cone_v}}° tall. A Lambertian backlight puts only {{lamb_frac}} of its flux inside a ±{{cone_design}}° cone and wastes the rest. Prism films narrow the cone and raise the on-axis luminance: 3M measures about 1.5 for one film and 2.8 for a stack of two crossed films with a reflective polariser, so the plan's assumed {{gain_film}} for the crossed pair is in range; a backlight built as a condenser and a weak engineered diffuser can reach several times more. The ideal gain for the design cone is {{collim_ideal}}×, which is why production backlights collimate. The plan builds the Lambertian-plus-films version first, because it is uniform and forgiving, and treats collimation as the optimisation if power or heat demand it.

Heat, and the panel's limits

Two things get hot. The LEDs turn most of their {{p_led}} into heat at the board, which a finned heat sink and a small fan remove through the housing's base. The cell absorbs the light it blocks: of the {{phi_out}} lm reaching it, about {{t_lcd}} passes, and the rest, about {{p_abs_cell}} of radiant power over {{a_crop_cm2}} cm², is absorbed in the polarisers and colour filters. That is {{irr_cell}} W/m², more than direct sunlight on the cell's own area, and a consumer cell is specified to about 70 °C. Three mitigations: the backlight runs at full power only when the ambient sensor says daylight; a thermistor on the cell's back lets the controller derate the backlight above a set temperature and blank it above a higher one; and the fan draws cabin air across the cell's rear. The acceptance test is a 30-minute run at full power in a 40 °C enclosure with the cell under the limit (section 10).

The cell also has a clearing temperature above which it goes dark. Typical liquid-crystal mixtures clear near 75 °C, which is why consumer panels darken in direct sun on a hot day; automotive mixtures clear above 100 °C, Kyocera's HUD panels are rated to 105 °C, and the industrial sunlight-readable modules are rated to 70 °C operating. This is the second reason the sun hazard of section 6.8 matters for a TFT, and the reason the plan tags the cell's temperature rating as verify for whichever panel is bought: the kit panel's rating is not published.

The black level at night

A liquid-crystal cell does not close completely. An IPS cell passes about one part in {{cr_panel}} of its backlight when black (assumed; measure). At the daylight setting that leak is {{black_day_eye}} cd/m² at the eye, which is invisible against a daytime scene. At night, with the backlight dimmed so white sits at the 50 cd/m² ceiling, the leak is {{black_night_eye}} cd/m² at the eye, which is a faint grey rectangle against a road lit at one or two cd/m² by the headlamps. The mitigation is the dimming curve itself: the backlight follows the ambient sensor down, and the plan sets the night white level at the ceiling, not above it. A VA cell has three times the contrast of IPS and is the alternative if the rectangle is visible in the Stage 2 night test. Local dimming is not available on a panel this size and is not needed.

The panel's own measurements, in order

  1. Stock luminance and uniformity with the luminance meter, then the stock backlight's luminance with the cell removed. Their ratio is the cell's transmittance and replaces the {{t_lcd}} assumption. A stock panel at {{stock_panel}} cd/m² over a {{stock_bl_example}} cd/m² backlight would confirm it.
  2. Polarisation axis of the panel's output, with a reference polariser.
  3. Contrast ratio, black against white, at the stock backlight.
  4. With the custom backlight: luminance on axis and at ±{{cone_design}}° horizontally and ±10° vertically; cell temperature against time at full power.
  5. Input lag of the HDMI board with the photodiode.

6.7 Three build stages

The optics are built in three stages. Each stage tests a piece of the final design, and the first two can be built before the custom mirror arrives. Stage 0 is a bench rig that tests software on a desk. Stage 1 places a combiner in the car and tests registration at a short virtual distance. Stage 2 adds the relay mirror and the windshield and is the demo target.

Stage 0: bench rig (weeks 1–3)

The monitor, or the 5.5-inch panel once it arrives, sits face-up in a black enclosure, and a 70/30 teleprompter beamsplitter is set at 45° above it. A camera, or the developer's eye, looks through the beamsplitter from the far side. The flat beamsplitter forms a virtual image of the panel at the same distance as the panel, about 0.6 m. Teleprompter glass uses a convention in which the first number is the transmitted share, so 30% of the panel's light is reflected toward the viewer; confirm the convention with the vendor (verify).

The purpose of Stage 0 is software: projection maths, colour and alpha choices, and latency instrumentation. It has no virtual distance and no windshield, so it tests the renderer rather than the optics. Teleprompter glass is sold in sizes of 300 mm and above by teleprompter suppliers, while the optics catalogues stop near 85 mm (verify).

Stage 1: Fresnel magnifier and in-car combiner (weeks 4–8)

Stage 1 moves the rig into the car, on the dash top. A 300 mm Fresnel lens sits between the panel and a combiner, a free-standing glass above the dash. The panel is 280 mm from the lens, which is inside the 300 mm focal length. An object inside the focal length of a converging lens produces a virtual image on the same side as the object, magnified. That is how a magnifying glass works, and it is how Stage 1 makes a virtual image.

1/v = 1/f − 1/u = 1/300 − 1/280 so v = −4.2 m (virtual, on the panel side) magnification M = |v| / u = 4200 / 280 = 15 image = 80 × 40 mm × 15 = 1.2 × 0.6 m so 16° × 8° as seen from the eye pixels: 80 mm of the 277 ppi monitor ≈ 872 px; 872 px ÷ 16° ≈ 54 px per degree

Fifty-four pixels per degree is close to the eye's limit, so the Stage 1 image is sharp enough to judge registration. Fresnel lenses add ring artefacts and some colour fringing, which is acceptable for a first in-car result and for night demos. Stage 1 leaves the windshield untouched and the combiner stands above the dash, which is the more exposed of the two in-car configurations legally (section 11.2). With the monitor as the panel, Stage 1 uses an 80 × 40 mm crop; with the 5.5-inch panel it uses the same crop and the same lens.

Stage 2: relay mirror into the windshield (weeks 6–12)

Stage 2 is the project's design and the demo target. The light path is TFT panel, cold-mirror fold, concave mirror, windshield, eye. The concave mirror does the magnification; the windshield is the final reflector and sits between the mirror and the driver. The fold mirror is a cold mirror, one that reflects visible light and transmits infrared, for a reason that belongs to the sun hazard and is explained in section 6.8.

The single-mirror calculation uses the mirror equation with f = {{f2}}. For a virtual image the object must lie inside the focal length. Setting the virtual image at 7.5 m gives an object distance of {{u2}}, a magnification of {{m2}}, an image of {{img2_w}} × {{img2_h}} at 7.5 m, and a field of {{fov2_w}}° × {{fov2_h}}°. The mirror's aperture follows from the eye box and the field: at an assumed 1 m from the eye along the folded path it is about {{mir_w}} × {{mir_h}} mm, which is why the plan quotes a 300 × 200 mm mirror.

The single-mirror calculation is still not enough to order a mirror. The windshield is a second reflecting surface between the concave mirror and the eye. Its curvature and its position change where the eye perceives the image, and neither is in the calculation. The ray trace that fixes f, u and the mirror's shape must include the windshield's actual curvature for the chosen car, the eye point, and the eye box. Until that ray trace exists, the mirror is a design estimate.

Sequencing consequence

The windshield's curvature depends on the car, so the car decision must come before the mirror is specified. The mirror order follows the ray trace, which follows the car. Section 9 shows where that lands on the schedule, and it is the critical path.

The mirror is a custom spherical first-surface mirror, about 300 × 200 mm, quoted by an optics shop; for a spherical mirror the radius of curvature is twice the focal length, so {{r2}}; no catalogue mirror is that size. A spherical mirror used off-axis has astigmatism and keystone, a distortion in which the image's top and bottom have different widths. The keystone is corrected in software, since hudd pre-distorts the image, and the residual astigmatism is accepted for the demo. A freeform mirror from an automotive HUD supplier is the stretch option.

6.8 The sun hazard

The concave mirror is a converging reflector. Sunlight that enters through the windshield and strikes it is focused toward its focal point, as a magnifying glass focuses the sun onto paper. For the Stage 2 geometry the focal point is {{f2}} from the mirror, and the panel sits at {{u2}}, {{focus_gap}} short of it. This section answers three questions the previous plan left open: how much power, from which direction, and what to do about it.

How much. A ray trace of parallel rays on a 300 × 200 mm spherical mirror of radius {{r2}} puts 90% of the rays within {{r90_pgu}} mm of the axis at the panel plane, a concentration of about {{conc_pgu}} times. The mirror's aperture collects about {{sun_w}} of direct sunlight at 1,000 W/m² after an assumed {{ws_t_sun}} windshield transmission, into a spot about {{sun_spot_mm}} mm across: {{sun_wcm2}} W/cm², before any filter. The cold-mirror fold transmits the infrared half of that to an absorber behind it and reflects only the visible, an assumed {{vis_frac}} of the power, leaving {{sun_after_cold}}; with an infrared-cut glass in front of the panel as well, TI's own worked budget for a HUD passes {{filt_frac}} of the solar power through its filter set, leaving {{sun_after_filt}}. An s-pass polariser sheet at the housing aperture passes the HUD's own s-polarised image unchanged and halves the unpolarised sunlight, leaving {{sun_after_pol}}: still {{sun_after_pol_wcm2}} W/cm², about {{sun_suns}} times direct sunlight, on a plastic polariser. Filters reduce the load; they do not remove the focus. This is the same mechanism the HUD makers' patents describe (a cold-coated fold mirror that passes infrared to a heat sink, a hot-mirror glass in front of the TFT), and one of them puts the time to damage its liquid-crystal cell, with its filters in place and at its own geometry, at just over {{sun_min_to_damage}} minutes; Hyundai Mobis showed a "sun burn-free" AR HUD at CES 2025 because wide-field AR HUDs concentrate more solar energy on the panel than conventional ones. The industry's own solar test (DIN 75220) runs about 765 W/m² for 200 hours.

Two published numbers put this design's spot in perspective. TI's solar-load study of a production HUD computes a peak of {{ti_peak}} kW/m² on the imager for a 7 m virtual image, after its filters, and measured a 45 °C rise on a BMW HUD's TFT at 12 kW/m². This design's spot after its filters is about {{sun_after_pol_kwm2}} kW/m²: {{sun_vs_ti}} times TI's peak and {{sun_vs_ti_tft}} times the level that produced the 45 °C rise. The difference is the mirror: a 300 mm sphere at f/1.1 with the panel {{focus_gap}} inside its focus puts the sun's image near its smallest blur, where a production freeform mirror spreads it. Two consequences. The panel cannot sit in the focused sun for more than seconds, so the shutter and the sun-watch rule are mandatory, not precautions. And the ray trace that specifies the mirror should also report the solar spot at the panel, because a small change in the mirror's figure or the panel's position can spread the spot several-fold at no cost to the image.

From where. Light reverses. The HUD's rays leave the mirror, reflect off the glass to the eye, and the part that transmits instead continues through the glass into the sky. Sunlight from that part of the sky retraces the path: through the glass, onto the mirror, onto the panel. For a windshield raked {{rake}}° above horizontal and an eye ray looking down {{lookdown}}° to the HUD patch, that direction is {{sun_elev}}° above the horizon behind the car (Appendix A.12). A more upright glass raises it: {{sun_elev_32}}° at 32°, {{sun_elev_35}}° at 35°; a flatter one lowers it to {{sun_elev_25}}° at 25°. The patch of sky that can reach the panel is about {{patch_w}}° × {{patch_h}}° (the eye box as seen from the glass, plus the field), and the sun crosses that width in about {{patch_minutes}} minutes if the car's heading is fixed. The hazard is therefore specific: a high sun behind the car, which is a summer midday driving away from the sun, or a parked car facing away from it for hours.

What to do. Five measures, in order of reliability.

  1. A shutter over the mirror aperture, closed whenever the HUD is off. A parked car is the long-exposure case, and a closed shutter makes it a non-event. A small servo on the controller board drives it; it fails closed.
  2. A sun-watch rule while driving. The Jetson knows the time, the position and the heading from GNSS, so it can compute the sun's direction in the car's frame and compare it with the entry patch from the ray trace. Inside the patch it closes the shutter, blanks the HUD, shows a status glyph, and logs the event. The patch is small and the sun moves slowly, so this happens rarely, and when it does the driver loses the HUD for the time it takes to turn.
  3. The cold-mirror fold, the panel-side infrared-cut glass and the aperture polariser, which together pass about an eighth of the solar power that enters the aperture ({{filt_frac}} through the two infrared filters, then {{pol_t}} through the polariser) at any time the shutter is open.
  4. A thermistor on the cell, which blanks the backlight and closes the shutter above a set temperature, as a backstop for everything above.
  5. The thermocouple test before any sun exposure of the assembled unit: the panel's temperature is logged with a thermocouple at the focus while the housing, without the panel, is pointed at the sun through the windshield, and the measured rise decides whether the measures above are sufficient for the exposure times the sun-watch rule allows.

The previous plan called for a "cold mirror in front of the diffuser", which was the wrong component in the wrong place. The cold mirror belongs at the fold, where it dumps infrared out of the path; a hot mirror or IR-cut glass belongs in front of the panel, where it keeps infrared off the cell. The plan uses both, as the patents do.

6.9 Packaging

The whole optical train sits in a 3D-printed, matte-black housing on the dash top. The mirror aperture is under a dust-cover glass at the base of the windshield, with the s-pass polariser laminated to its inside and the shutter beneath it. The panel and its backlight sit at the rear of the housing, lowest, with the heat sink and fan venting through the housing's base; the cold-mirror fold sits above them with a black absorber behind it; the concave mirror sits at the front. The housing comes off without tools so the car can return to stock, which matters for the installation sign-off (section 11). Matte black inside the housing limits stray light, which would otherwise appear as haze or a faint second image.

The road camera is not in the housing. It mounts high on the windshield's centreline for the reason given in section 5.5, with its cable run along the headliner to the Jetson. The driver camera sits on the dash or the column shroud, aimed at the driver's face, with its infrared illuminator for night. The GNSS antenna sits on the dash top near the glass.

6.10 Calibration

Three transforms take a point from the car's frame to a pixel on the panel. Each is measured once and stored in a file on the Jetson, except the first, which the head tracker adjusts live. Figure 23 shows the chain and how each step is measured.

  1. Car frame to eye. The car frame has its origin at the road camera's left lens, x forward, y right, z down, with the ground plane measured by stereo each frame. A fixed translation takes it to the nominal eye point, measured with a tape and refined with a target board at 30 m. The head tracker supplies a live offset from the nominal point, in the driver camera's frame, which is itself tied to the car frame by a one-time measurement of the driver camera's position and aim.
  2. Eye to virtual display plane. A pinhole intrinsic for the virtual image. Its focal length in pixels is half the crop height divided by the tangent of half the vertical field of view. It is measured by putting a camera at the eye point and photographing a rendered grid against a real grid on the ground.
  3. Display pre-distortion. A two-dimensional mesh warp that cancels the mirror's and windshield's curvature. It is fitted from the same photograph and applied as a final full-screen pass in the renderer, not folded into the matrix.

The calibration depends on the windshield, so it must be redone for every car and every windshield change, and the camera-to-car part must be redone if the road camera is remounted. It is part of the Stage 2 re-calibration in Phase 6 (section 9).
