1Recommendation in brief

Build the HUD as a self-contained instrument on one computer. The Jetson Orin NX captures a stereo view of the road, finds the lane lines and the vehicles, people and signs in it, measures its own speed and turn rate from a GNSS receiver and an inertial unit, tracks the driver's head, and draws a registered overlay on a small TFT LCD panel. A concave mirror relays the panel's picture into the windshield, where the driver sees it as a virtual image 7.5 m ahead. A person drives the car throughout; the HUD never touches the car.

The design is organised around three facts. First, a windshield reflects polarised light twenty times more strongly in one orientation than the other, and a liquid-crystal panel emits polarised light, so the panel is oriented to the windshield and no polariser is wasted. Second, luminance is conserved through a mirror, so daylight visibility is decided at the panel: an ordinary panel at a few hundred cd/m² cannot reach the target, and the plan's answer is a custom LED backlight behind the small region of the panel that is actually used. Third, registration depends on where the camera is relative to the eye more than on how accurately it measures distance, so the road camera is mounted close to the driver's line of sight.

Why not keep openpilot as a sensor?

The previous plan's reason for the comma four was driving. Without driving, the comma four could still serve as a calibrated sensor head: this checkout has a passive mode in which the car interface parses the bus but never transmits (selfdrive/car/card.py lines 113–117 set the panda's safety model to noOutput when openpilot's toggle is off, checked). That option is kept in section 5.7 and it is not the recommendation, for three reasons. It costs $1,098 (the comma shop's price as read on 2026-10-08; not re-checked) and ties the project to openpilot's supported-car list and harnesses, for a car that will not be driven by it. It keeps the whole openpilot runtime in the loop for a demo that uses four of its outputs. And the project's own description says the camera neural network runs on the Jetson; this plan does that.

Why a TFT LCD, and what it costs

A laser DLP projector on a diffuser was chosen before because it was the only source class with daylight headroom after a 24% windshield reflection. A TFT panel reaches the same place by a different route. Its light is already polarised, so the 42% polariser loss of the projector path does not exist. Its brightness is set by its backlight, which can be built to the luminance needed, and dimmed by pulse width over the whole range from noon to night. It has no speckle, no diffuser, no fan, no input-lag unknown, and it is the technology every production HUD uses. The costs are real too: an off-the-shelf panel delivers about {{stock_pct}} of the sky target through the windshield; the backlight that fixes that must be designed, built and kept cool; and a liquid-crystal cell has a black level, so the HUD window glows faintly at night unless the backlight follows the ambient light. Sections 6.5 and 6.6 give the budget and the design.

2Background for readers new to the project

This chapter explains the ideas the rest of the plan relies on: what a head-up display does, where its image appears and why that matters, what registration means, and what it means that this HUD perceives the road itself. A reader who already knows these can skip to chapter 3.

2.1 What a head-up display does

A head-up display (HUD) puts information in the driver's line of sight so the driver does not have to look down at a dashboard. There are two design questions. First, where the image appears to be, meaning its focus distance. Second, how large it is, meaning its field of view, the angle it covers as seen from the eye.

An augmented-reality HUD goes further: it places each graphic where the thing it describes is. A ribbon drawn on the lane, or a box drawn around a pedestrian, only works if the overlay stays on the object as the car moves and the driver's head moves. The technical word for that is registration, covered in section 2.3.

2.2 Where the image appears

Light from a display reaches the eye as a bundle of rays. If the rays diverge as if they came from a point 7.5 m away, the eye perceives the image at 7.5 m. Such an image is called a virtual image: no light actually originates there, but the eye cannot tell. A virtual image is the mechanism that lets a HUD draw content at road distance.

The eye focuses on whatever it is looking at, a process called accommodation. A screen 0.6 m away forces the eye to refocus when the driver looks up at the road, which can take a fraction of a second and causes eye strain. A virtual image at 7.5 m is in the range where road objects sit, so the eye can keep one focus across the road and the graphics.

The plan's convention is that a road point at distance d is drawn where the line of sight crosses a plane at distance D. On a flat road with the eye at height h = 1.22 m, the crossing is at height h(1 − D/d) above the road. With D = 7.5 m, a point 10 m away is drawn 0.31 m above the road on the plane, and a point 30 m away is drawn 0.92 m above it. Far points therefore sit higher on the plane, and their marks shift less when the head moves.

2.3 Registration and conformal graphics

Registration error is the angle between where a graphic is drawn and where the thing it marks actually is, measured from the eye. A one-degree error at 30 m is about half a metre on the road. The plan calls graphics that must register conformal, and graphics that stay fixed non-conformal. Each element in the demo is one or the other.

Registration is the reason the rest of the plan is so detailed. A ribbon edge that is 1° off at 30 m looks wrong to a driver who is watching the lane paint, even though the same error would be invisible on a dashboard gauge.

2.4 What "no self-driving" changes

In the previous plan the HUD was a subscriber to openpilot, which was both the driver and the perception system. Every drawn element was a copy of something openpilot had already computed for its own purpose, and the vehicle's speed and turn rate came from the car's bus. In this plan there is no such source. The HUD has to perceive the road for itself, which means three new jobs: finding lane lines in an image and placing them on the ground, finding objects and measuring how far away they are, and measuring the car's own motion without a connection to the car.

Two consequences follow. There is no planned path, because nobody is planning: the hero element becomes the lane itself, drawn as a ribbon between the detected lines. And the car no longer needs to be a particular car. Any car with a dash top that can carry the housing will do, and the installation is reversible without tools.

A third consequence is subtler. openpilot's model output was already in three dimensions in the car's frame. A lane network outputs points in the image, and lifting them to the road needs either a known camera-to-ground geometry or a measured one. The plan uses a stereo camera and fits the ground plane from its depth each frame, so the lift does not depend on a calibration that drifts as the car pitches (section 5.2).

2.5 What the system does not do

  • It does not steer, brake, accelerate or warn the car's own systems. It has no wire to any bus and no actuator.
  • It does not know the car's speed from the car. Speed comes from satellites and an inertial unit, which is accurate while moving and reads zero when stopped.
  • It does not navigate. Turn-by-turn arrows are listed as a stretch goal in section 11.3 and are not part of the demo.
  • It does not replace the driver's attention. Hazard outlines are drawn because they are a convincing demonstration of registration, not because the driver should rely on them.
