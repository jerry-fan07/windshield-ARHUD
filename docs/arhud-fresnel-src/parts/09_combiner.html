9The combiner, and how bright the image is

A combiner is a sheet of glass that is partly a window and partly a mirror. The plan uses 70/30 teleprompter glass: a coating on one face reflects 30% of the light that meets it and lets 70% through. Tilted above the lens, it does two jobs at once (Figure [[N combiner]]A). Light from the road passes through it to the eye, dimmed to 70%. Light from the lens below reflects off it toward the eye, 30% of it. Both arrive along the same line of sight, so the graphics appear to hang over the road: the two pictures are combined. The rest of the HUD light (70%) carries on upward through the glass and is lost, and 30% of the road light reflects away.

The ghost from the back surface

The coating is on one face; the other face is plain glass and reflects too, about {{r_back}}% at this angle for unpolarised light. That second reflection comes from slightly further along, so it makes a second, fainter copy of the HUD, shifted by twice the glass thickness times a geometric factor (Figure [[N combiner]]B). For 3 mm glass the copies are {{gh_off_3}} mm apart, which at {{d_eye_img}} m is {{gh_am_3}}′, about two pixels; for 6 mm glass, {{gh_am_6}}′. The ghost carries about {{ghost_rel}}% of the main image's brightness. This is the same physics as the windshield ghost the plan analyses for Stage 2, at a much smaller scale because the combiner is thin and the image is far.

The LCD's light is polarised, which changes both reflections. At {{i_comb}}° the plain back face reflects {{rs_comb}}% of light polarised one way (s, vibrating parallel to the glass surface) and only {{rp_comb}}% of the other (p). Turning the panel, or adding a half-wave film, so its light arrives p-polarised would nearly remove the back-surface ghost, but the coating's 30% is also specified for unpolarised light and may drop for p. Measure the main reflection and the ghost with the actual panel at the combiner, in both orientations, and choose. PMMA's low birefringence matters here: a polycarbonate lens would scramble the polarisation unevenly and make the result patchy. Polarised sunglasses can black out the HUD entirely, as the plan notes for Stage 2.

Brightness at the eye

Section 3 explained that the lens does not change luminance except for its losses. So the brightness at the eye is the panel's luminance times three factors (Figure [[N luminance]]A): the lens's two surfaces ({{T_axis}} on axis), losses at the grooves and draft walls ({{draft_loss}}, ASSUMED near the centre; up to {{dim_edge}} at the field edge, section 8), and the combiner's 30%. Together, {{path1}}. The plan's 4K monitor at 1,200 cd/m² gives {{lum_0}} cd/m² at the eye; the 5.5-inch kit's stock backlight gives {{lum_1}}; the custom backlight's design 10,000 cd/m² gives {{lum_2}}.

Against the road (Figure [[N luminance]]B), any of the three is far too bright at night, about 1 cd/m², and must be dimmed: the custom backlight by {{night_dim}}× to reach the plan's 50 cd/m² night ceiling. In overcast daylight the monitor and the custom backlight are readable; the stock kit is marginal. With the custom backlight, Stage 1 would even stay above the threshold against a sunlit road, partly because the combiner dims the road to 70% while reflecting 30% of the HUD's light, where Stage 2's windshield reflects only 24%. Brightness is therefore not what limits Stage 1 to night and overcast use. Sunlight is.
