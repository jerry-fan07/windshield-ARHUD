8Rings, ghosts and glare

Section 5 showed that the grooves themselves blur away. What the eye can still notice comes from light taking the wrong path, and almost all of it starts at a draft wall or at a surface reflection. At night, with bright symbols on a black background, these are the artefacts a passenger will point at.

Sight lines that meet a draft wall

Figure [[N stray]] traces sight lines backward from the eye into the real teeth at the edge of the field. Most enter a facet and continue to the intended pixel. Those that arrive near the foot of a tooth meet the next draft wall instead, or are turned by one inside the tooth. They refract at a steep angle, are trapped by total internal reflection at the flat face, and either come out far across the lens or never leave the sheet. At the field edge {{eyeclean_90}} of the sight lines are clean, {{eyefar_90}} end on a panel spot far from the intended pixel, and {{eyelost_90}} are trapped; at 2.5° from the centre {{eyeclean_30}} are clean.

Three consequences follow.

  • An even dimming toward the edges. The wall zones repeat with every groove, so the eye's blur spreads them into a smooth loss: the field edge is about {{dim_edge}} darker than the centre. The fraction does not depend on the pitch, because finer grooves are also proportionally shallower. A brightness ramp in the renderer can flatten it if it shows.
  • Faint smears from bright spots elsewhere. The "far" sight lines see a panel spot well away from the one they should. If that spot is lit, its light appears where it should not, arranged around the lens centre, which is why Fresnel artefacts look like arcs, rings and radial streaks. The remedy is to keep everything outside the graphics black: the crop's backlight only behind the crop, the rest of the panel and the box interior matte black.
  • Dust and scratches on the grooved face scatter in the same way. The grooves face up in Stage 1, so a closed lid when the HUD is off and a soft brush, not a cloth, keep them clean.

Reflection ghosts

Every uncoated surface reflects about 4% of the light that meets it head-on, more at slant. Light from the panel that reflects off the grooved face, back down, and off the flat face again leaves the lens a second time on a different path and forms a faint, displaced copy of the bright graphics. In the renders these ghosts appear in the corners and as dim continuations of the lane lines; raising the exposure sixteen-fold makes them obvious (Figure [[N thr_ghost]]). Measured in the render, a ghost of a thin white line is about {{ghost_line}} as bright as the line, and the ghost of a large filled area like the ribbon about {{ghost_area}} of that area's brightness; black parts of the picture glow at about {{veil_black}} of white from scattered light. That is invisible by day and faint at night, but noticeable against black if the HUD is turned up. An anti-reflection coating, which Knight Optical offers on its stock lenses, reduces each surface's 4% to well under 1% and the ghosts by more than an order of magnitude; it is worth having if Stage 1 is shown at night.

Moiré

Two fine regular patterns laid over each other can produce a coarse beat pattern. Here the candidates are the grooves (2.5′ apart from the eye) and the panel's pixels (0.7–1′ apart). The eye render shows no moiré: the eye's blur over 7 or more grooves removes the groove pattern before it can beat with anything. (The pinhole render, which sees the grooves sharply, does show striped patterns where a ghost overlaps them.) If it ever appears, for example on a camera recording the HUD with a small aperture, changing the panel distance slightly or rotating the lens a few degrees moves or removes it.
