10Sunlight through the lens

A 300 mm lens with a 300 mm focal length is, among other things, an excellent burning glass. Pointed at the sun, it gathers the light falling on 0.09 m² and brings it to a point. In Stage 1 the panel sits {{sun_focus_beyond}} mm short of that point, which is close enough to matter. The plan treats the sun hazard carefully for Stage 2 (cold-mirror fold, infrared filter, polariser, shutter and a sun-watch rule, section 6.8 of the plan). Stage 1 has none of these, and the plan does not discuss its sun exposure.

Sun straight down the lens

With the sun on the lens axis, after the windshield's 70% and the combiner's 70% (both ASSUMED), {{sun_W}} W reaches the lens ({{sun_W_nocomb}} W if the combiner is not above it). Traced through the real facets, it lands on the panel as a square spot with 90% of the power within {{sun_r90}} mm of its centre, peaking at {{sun_peak}} kW/m², about {{sun_peak_suns}} times full sunlight (Figure [[N sunspot]]). For comparison, Texas Instruments designs production HUDs for at most 40 kW/m² on the picture generator, and the plan cites a BMW HUD whose TFT warmed 45 °C at 12 kW/m². An LCD's front polariser absorbs about half of the light that hits it, so roughly 20 W would be dumped into a few square centimetres of the panel.

Sun off the axis: worse, not better

Tilting the sun moves the spot across the panel: about {{sun_x_10}} mm at 10° and {{sun_x_20}} mm at 20°. One might expect it to spread as it moves. It does at large angles, but first it sharpens (Figure [[N sunangle]]A). Off the axis the lens's focal surface curves toward it (the same field curvature as in section 4, seen from the other side), and around 12° it crosses the panel plane. There the sun is nearly in focus on the panel: the traced peak reaches {{sun_worst}} kW/m² at {{sun_worst_ang}}° (in 1 mm bins), enough to burn through a polariser in seconds and to char most plastics. Up to about 28° the spot still lands on the 16-inch monitor, and up to 12° on the 5.5-inch panel.

How often does that happen? With the lens lying flat on the dash, its axis points straight up, so what matters is the sun's angle from overhead. At solar noon in June it is 2° at 25° latitude, 12° at 35° and 22° at 45° (Figure [[N sunangle]]B). The windshield's rake lets that light in from the front, and the combiner passes 70% of it. A parked car with an uncovered rig will, on a summer day, sweep the spot across the panel. A second path exists too: sunlight from behind and above can reflect off the combiner (30%) straight down the HUD's own light path.

Rules for Stage 1

1. An opaque lid over the lens, closed whenever the HUD is not in use and always when parked. This is the primary defence, and it also keeps dust out of the upward-facing grooves.

2. Never leave the rig in a parked car in the sun, even covered: the dash can pass PMMA's 65–95 °C service limit and warp the lens.

3. Extend the plan's sun-watch rule (sun position from GNSS time, position and heading) to Stage 1 with the patch of Figure [[N sunangle]]A, and close the lid and blank the panel when the sun enters it.

4. Put the plan's thermistor on the back of the panel in Stage 1 as well, with the same cut-off.

5. Before any daytime drive, repeat the plan's thermocouple test with the empty box in the sun.
