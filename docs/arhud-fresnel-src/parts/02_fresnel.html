2What a Fresnel lens is

An ordinary lens bends light with its curved surfaces: where the surface is tilted, light crossing it changes direction. All the bending happens at the surface; the glass inside only carries the light through. A Fresnel lens keeps the surface and drops most of the inside.

Figure [[N collapse]] shows the idea with this lens's real numbers. A solid lens with a {{f}} mm focal length and a 300 mm diameter would be a dome {{asph_sag_edge}} mm thick, weighing about {{bulk_kg}} kg in PMMA. (A simple spherical surface cannot even reach 300 mm: its radius would be {{r_pcx}} mm, so it closes into a hemisphere at {{pcx_max_d}} mm. The surface has to be aspheric.) Cut that dome into thin rings, keep only the slanted top of each ring, and lower every piece onto a thin base. What remains is a {{t}} mm sheet weighing {{sheet_g}} g with {{n_grooves_edge}} concentric teeth from the centre to the edge, each with the same slope the solid lens had at that radius.

The parts of a tooth

Each tooth has two faces (Figure [[N anatomy]]). The facet is the slanted face that does the work: its angle to the sheet equals the slope of the solid lens at that radius. The draft wall is the near-vertical step back up to the next tooth. It is there only because the surface has to return to the base; light that hits it goes somewhere it should not, which is the source of most Fresnel artefacts (section 8). The spacing of the teeth is the pitch, {{pitch}} mm on the plan's lens, and it is the same everywhere, so the teeth get deeper toward the edge, where the facets are steeper.

The facet angles come from a calculation, not a catalogue: the manufacturer does not publish the profile. Stock Fresnel lenses are designed so that a parallel beam arriving on the grooved side comes to a sharp focus on the flat side (an ASSUMED design, standard for stock lenses), and solving that condition ray by ray gives a unique facet angle at each radius (Appendix A.2). Every number and figure in this document uses that profile.

One ray, followed through

Figure [[N onefacet]] follows a single ray from the centre of the panel through the lens at r = 100 mm. Two refractions do the bending. Entering the flat face, the ray slows down in the plastic and bends toward the perpendicular; leaving through the facet, it speeds up again and bends away from the facet's perpendicular, which is tilted {{fx_g}}°. The rule at each surface is Snell's law, n1 sin θ1 = n2 sin θ2, with n = 1 in air and {{n_d}} in PMMA. The ray arrives at {{fx_air}}° from the axis and leaves at {{fx_out}}°: almost parallel to the axis, and spreading slightly outward, exactly as if it had come from a point {{v_m}} m behind the lens. That is the virtual image of section 3.

Why PMMA

Nearly every large Fresnel lens is moulded or cast in PMMA (acrylic), including the two lenses the plan lists. It transmits about 92% of visible light (two surfaces), it bends colours apart only a little more than optical glass (Abbe number {{abbe_pmma}} against {{abbe_bk7}} for BK7 glass and {{abbe_pc}} for polycarbonate, section 6), and it has low internal stress, so it barely disturbs the LCD's polarised light (section 9). Its weaknesses are heat and scratches. PMMA softens near 96 °C and is rated for continuous use at 65–95 °C, below what a dashboard reaches in a parked car in summer, so the lens should not be left on the dash in the sun (section 10 adds a second reason). It also expands about ten times as much as glass with temperature, which moves the image slightly (section 4). Clean it with water and a soft cloth; ammonia and alcohol cleaners can craze it.
