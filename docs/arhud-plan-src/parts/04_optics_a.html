6Optics

The optics are the hardest part of the plan, and the part where the monitors alone will not get the job done. The README commits to projection onto a relay mirror. This chapter designs that path and adds two cheaper stages before it, so software work is never blocked on glass. Each subsection explains the physics before the numbers, and Appendix A derives every formula used.

6.1 The constraints

Six constraints define the optical design. Each is a requirement on what the driver sees, not on a component.

The contrast ratio in the plan is defined as (overlay + background) ÷ background. With a background of 10,000 cd/m² and a ratio of 1.15, the overlay must add 0.15 × 10,000 = 1,500 cd/m², which is where the noon target comes from. The definition matters because a different one, such as the ratio of overlay to background, would give a different target.

6.2 Window geometry

A field of view is an angle, and the physical size of the image depends on the distance it is drawn at. At a virtual image distance of 7.5 m, the window's width is 2 × 7.5 m × tan(θ/2), where θ is the field of view. The window's aspect ratio, the width divided by the height, must match the picture generator's aspect ratio. The PGU is 60 × 30 mm, which is 2:1. 6.3 Reflection off glass, and why the source must be s-polarised

When light meets a glass surface, part is transmitted and part is reflected, and the fraction reflected depends on the angle and on the light's polarisation. Polarisation has two components. The s component is perpendicular to the plane of incidence; the p component is parallel to it. For glass with a refractive index of 1.52, the Fresnel equations give the reflected fraction for each. Figure 11 plots them against the angle of incidence.

At the plan's design point of 65°, the s component reflects {{rs65}} of its light and the p component reflects {{rp65}}. The difference is the design decision. The windshield is a reflector for the HUD, so the picture has to be s-polarised to be bright, or the projector's output has to be converted to s-polarisation with a film. The laser DLP projector's output may already be polarised; its polarisation has to be measured before the film is ordered. Revision 2 said the same thing without showing the curve.

Two consequences of the curve were not in revision 2.

  • The monitor is already polarised. An LCD panel emits linearly polarised light through its front polariser. The orientation in which the monitor is mounted therefore decides whether the windshield sees s-polarised light and reflects {{rs65}} of it, or p-polarised light and reflects {{rp65}}: a twenty-fold difference in brightness from a rotation of the panel. Before the monitor is used as the night picture generator in Stage 2, its polarisation axis must be measured against the plane of incidence, and the panel rotated or a half-wave retarder film added so the light arrives s-polarised. The Stage 0 and Stage 1 combiners are coated beamsplitters whose reflection can also depend on polarisation, and the plan has no figures for them (verify vendor), so the same orientation check is done there first.
  • The diffuser scrambles the projector's polarisation. A rear-projection diffuser depolarises the light that passes through it, so whatever the laser DLP emits, the light leaving the PGU is close to unpolarised. The polariser sheet in the parts list is therefore needed, not optional, and it passes only about {{pol_t}} of unpolarised light (an assumed figure for an iodine film polariser; a wire-grid polariser passes a little more). The budget in section 6.5 now carries that loss as its last row.

The angle of 65° is the plan's design incidence on the windshield. The real angle depends on the eye point and the mirror position, and the ray trace in Stage 2 (section 6.6) will confirm it. If the real angle differs a great deal, the reflected fraction changes with it, and the luminance budget in section 6.5 must be recomputed.

6.4 The ghost image and the wedge interlayer

A laminated windshield has two reflecting surfaces, the outer and the inner, separated by the plastic interlayer. Each reflects the picture, so the driver sees two images. In a plain laminate the two surfaces are parallel, so the two images are displaced sideways by a fixed amount. That is the ghost image. A wedge-shaped interlayer tilts the inner surface by a small angle, and the two reflections then leave the glass at different angles. Choosing the wedge so that the two reflections meet at the design eye point makes the ghost disappear at that point.

The wedge removes the ghost only at the nominal eye height. Above or below it, the two images separate again, and the driver sees a double image when the head moves. For this reason the plan treats the wedge as the fix for the nominal case and the eye-box test as the check for the rest. The wedge angle is set by the glass maker, and this plan does not quote one. That is also why the plan favours a car that has a factory head-up display trim: a wedge-PVB windshield for that trim is more likely to exist.

6.5 The luminance budget

Luminance, measured in cd/m² or nits, is the brightness of a surface as seen by the eye. It is conserved by an ideal imaging system: a mirror that magnifies an image makes it bigger, not brighter. So the budget is a chain of multipliers. Each reflection or transmission multiplies the source luminance by a fraction, and the result is the luminance at the eye. The source luminance of a laser DLP is computed from its light output and the diffuser's area. For a Lambertian transmissive diffuser that is L = Φη ÷ (πA), where Φ is the projector's output in lumens, η is the diffuser's transmission, and A is its area. For 2,000 lm, η = 50%, and a 60 × 30 mm diffuser, that gives {{L_DLP}} cd/m² at the source.

The dimming requirement follows from the two thresholds. From the 1,500 cd/m² noon target down to the 50 cd/m² night ceiling is a ratio of {{dim_day_night}}, which is what the revision 2 plan should have said. If the projector runs at its full output, {{dlp_ws}} cd/m² at the eye, the range from full output down to 50 cd/m² is {{dim_full_night}}. The daylight target needs only about {{dlp_day_frac}} of the projector's full output on the realistic path with mirrors and polariser (1,500 ÷ {{dlp_ws_mirr_pol}}), and {{dlp_day_frac_raw}} on the bare windshield path, so the realistic working range is the first figure. Whether the projector can be dimmed that far is not known: many laser projectors do not dim below roughly a fifth of full output by source control alone, and the floor of the chosen model must be measured (verify). If it cannot reach the daylight working point, a fixed neutral-density filter sets that point and the switchable filter takes it to night. The plan specifies projector dimming plus a switchable neutral-density filter for night.

The monitors do not reach the daylight target, at about {{yod_pct}} of it through the windshield path, which is why they are used only for the bench rig and for night driving. The laser DLP reaches daylight with headroom, which is why it is the Stage 2 picture generator, and why the sun hazard in section 6.7 matters so much.
